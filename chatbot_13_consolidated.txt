Natural Language Processing (NLP)
Natural Language Processing (NLP) is a branch of artificial intelligence (AI) that focuses on the interaction between 
computers and humans through natural language. The goal of NLP is to enable machines to read, understand, interpret, and 
generate human language in a valuable and meaningful way.
NLP combines techniques from computational linguistics, machine learning, and deep learning to process and analyze 
large amounts of natural language data. It powers applications like chatbots, virtual assistants, translation systems, 
sentiment analysis, and more.
Key Components of NLP
NLP can be broadly divided into two major subfields:
1.
Natural Language Understanding (NLU) – Understanding the meaning behind the text.
2.
Natural Language Generation (NLG) – Producing human-like text.
Natural Language Understanding (NLU)
Natural Language Understanding (NLU) involves enabling machines to comprehend the structure and meaning of human 
language. It deals with extracting information from text and understanding its intent and context.

--- Page 2 ---
Goals of NLU
●
Recognize the intent behind the input.
●
Extract key entities and relationships.
●
Handle language variability (e.g., synonyms, ambiguity).
●
Understand sentiment and tone.
Core Tasks in NLU
1.
Tokenization: Breaking text into smaller units (words, phrases, or subwords).
○
Example:
Input: "I love programming."
Tokens: ["I", "love", "programming", "."]
2.
Morphological Analysis: Identifying the structure of words (e.g., root words, prefixes, suffixes).
○
Example:
"Running" → Root: "run", Suffix: "-ing"
3.
Part-of-Speech (POS) Tagging: Assigning grammatical categories to words.
○
Example:
"I love programming."
POS Tags: "I (pronoun), love (verb), programming (noun)"

--- Page 3 ---
4. Named Entity Recognition (NER): Identifying and classifying entities like names, locations, dates, etc.
●
Example:
"Elon Musk founded SpaceX in 2002."
Entities: "Elon Musk (Person), SpaceX (Organization), 2002 (Date)"
5. Syntactic Analysis (Parsing): Analyzing the grammatical structure of sentences.
●
Dependency Parsing: Identifying how words are connected.
●
Constituency Parsing: Dividing sentences into subphrases (NP, VP, etc.).
6. Semantic Analysis: Understanding the meaning of the text.
●
Word Sense Disambiguation (WSD): Identifying the correct meaning of a word based on context.
Example: "The bank of the river" vs. "A savings bank."
●
Semantic Role Labeling (SRL): Determining who did what to whom, when, and how.
7. Coreference Resolution: Identifying when two expressions refer to the same entity.
●
Example:
"Mary loves her dog. She takes it on walks."
("She" refers to "Mary", "it" refers to "dog")

--- Page 4 ---
8. Intent Recognition: Understanding the user’s purpose behind a query.
●
Example: "Book a flight to Paris." → Intent: Flight booking.
9. Sentiment Analysis: Determining the emotional tone behind a piece of text (positive, negative, neutral).
●
Example:
"I love this product!" → Sentiment: Positive.
Challenges in NLU
●
Ambiguity: Words or phrases can have multiple interpretations.
○
Example: "I saw a bat." (Animal or sports equipment?)
●
Sarcasm and Irony: Implicit meanings are difficult to detect.
●
Context Understanding: Requires grasping long-term dependencies across sentences.
●
Language Variability: Different dialects, slang, and grammar variations.

Natural Language Generation (NLG)
Natural Language Generation (NLG) is the process of creating coherent, contextually appropriate, and grammatically 
accurate text from structured or unstructured data.
Goals of NLG
●
Convert data into natural language.
●
Produce fluent and contextually appropriate responses.
●
Personalize output based on user input.
Core Tasks in NLG
1.
Content Planning: Deciding what information to present.
○
Example: In a weather report, decide to include temperature, humidity, and location.
2.
Text Structuring: Organizing the content into a logical flow.
○
Example: "Today's weather: It's sunny with a high of 25°C."
3.
Lexical Choice: Selecting the right words or phrases.
○
Example: Using "chilly" instead of "cold" for a casual tone.
4.
Surface Realization: Forming grammatically correct sentences.
○
Example: Data: {temperature: 25°C, condition: sunny}
Output: "It is sunny with a temperature of 25°C."

--- Page 6 ---
Referring Expression Generation: Properly identifying entities.
●
Example: Use "she" instead of repeating "Mary" multiple times.
Contextual Coherence: Ensuring the generated text fits the broader conversation.
●
Example: If the user asks, "What's the weather?" and then "Will I need an umbrella?" – the system must infer the 
second question refers to the weather forecast.
Types of NLG Systems
1.
Template-Based NLG: Pre-defined templates with placeholders for dynamic data.
○
Example: "Hello, [name]! Your balance is [amount]."
2.
Rule-Based NLG: Uses linguistic rules to generate text.
○
Example: "If temperature > 30, say 'It's hot today.'"
3.
Statistical and Neural NLG: Machine learning models generate text based on patterns from large datasets.
○
Example: GPT models generate diverse and creative responses.

--- Page 7 ---
Applications of NLG
●
Chatbots & Virtual Assistants: Conversational AI systems like Siri, Alexa.
●
Data Summarization: Summarizing long documents or reports.
●
Automated Reporting: Generating financial or weather reports.
●
Language Translation: Converting text between languages.
Challenges in NLG
●
Maintaining Coherence: Long outputs may lose logical flow.
●
Bias in Language Models: Training data biases may reflect in outputs.
●
Controlling Output Style: Difficulty adjusting formality or tone.
●
Hallucination: Generating plausible but factually incorrect information.

Steps of Natural Language Processing (NLP)
Lexical Analysis (Tokenization & Morphological Analysis)
Lexical analysis is the first step in NLP where raw text is broken down into smaller units (tokens) like words, subwords, 
or phrases. This step also involves analyzing the structure of these words (morphological analysis).
Goals of Lexical Analysis:
●
Divide text into meaningful units (tokens).
●
Identify linguistic properties like word stems, prefixes, and suffixes.
Key Processes:
1.
Tokenization: Splitting the input into words or subwords.
Example:
Input: "I love programming."
Tokens: ["I", "love", "programming", "."]
2.
Lemmatization and Stemming: Reducing words to their base or root form.
Example:
○
Stemming: "running" → "run"
○
Lemmatization: "better" → "good"

--- Page 9 ---
3.   Morphological Analysis: Recognizing the internal structure of words (e.g., prefixes, suffixes).
      Example: "unhappiness" = "un-" (negation) + "happy" (root) + "-ness" (noun-forming suffix).
Syntactic Analysis (Parsing)
Syntactic analysis, also known as parsing, involves analyzing the grammatical structure of a sentence. It checks if a 
sentence adheres to the rules of a formal grammar (syntax).
Goals of Syntactic Analysis:
●
Understand sentence structure.
●
Identify relationships between words (subject, verb, object).
●
Detect grammatical errors.

--- Page 10 ---
Key Processes:
1.
Parsing: Analyzing how words relate based on grammar rules.
○
Dependency Parsing: Identifying dependencies between words.
Example:
Sentence: "John eats an apple."
Dependencies: "eats" (root verb) → "John" (subject), "apple" (object).
○

NLP Aids or Tools
These are specialized resources and systems that help in processing and analyzing human language at various stages of 
Natural Language Processing (NLP). Each system focuses on a different layer of language understanding—from 
recognizing words to interpreting complex meanings in context.
Lexicon or Dictionary
A lexicon in NLP is a structured collection of words and their linguistic properties. It contains information such as word 
definitions, part of speech (POS), synonyms, antonyms, and even semantic relationships. These dictionaries help 
machines interpret and understand words in the correct context.
For example, if an NLP model encounters the word "bank", the lexicon helps the system decide whether it refers to a 
financial institution or a riverbank based on surrounding words. Lexicons also store morphological details like base 
forms (e.g., "running" → "run").
Example Tools:
●
WordNet: A large, lexical database where words are grouped into sets of synonyms called synsets, which capture 
relationships like hypernyms (broader terms) and hyponyms (specific terms).
●
UMLS (Unified Medical Language System): Specialized for medical and clinical terms.

--- Page 15 ---
Morphological Analysis System
Morphological analysis involves breaking down words into their smallest meaningful units, called morphemes (e.g., 
roots, prefixes, and suffixes). This system helps machines understand word formation, inflections, and derivations—how 
words change form to express different meanings.
For instance, the word "unhappiness" can be broken down as:
●
un- (prefix: negation)
●
happy (root word)
●
-ness (suffix: indicates a noun).
Morphological analysis is essential for tasks like lemmatization (reducing words to their dictionary form) and stemming 
(removing affixes to get the word's root). It improves performance in search engines and information retrieval by 
normalizing text.
Example Tools:
●
SpaCy: Provides efficient lemmatization and morphological tagging.
●
NLTK (Natural Language Toolkit): Performs both stemming and lemmatization for English and other languages.
●
Stanza: A multi-lingual NLP toolkit for robust morphological analysis.

Syntactic Analysis System
Also called parsing, syntactic analysis deals with sentence structure by examining how words are grammatically related. 
It helps machines identify the syntax rules that govern a language and detect relationships like subject-verb-object.
For example, in the sentence "The cat sleeps," a syntactic analysis system identifies:
●
NP (Noun Phrase): "The cat" (subject).
●
VP (Verb Phrase): "sleeps" (predicate).
There are two primary forms of syntactic analysis:
1.
Dependency Parsing: Identifies direct relationships between words (e.g., which word depends on another).
2.
Constituency Parsing: Breaks a sentence into nested phrases (e.g., noun phrases, verb phrases).
Example Tools:
●
Stanford Parser: Performs both constituency and dependency parsing.
●
SyntaxNet: Google's deep-learning-based syntactic analysis tool.
●
SpaCy: Provides fast and robust dependency parsing.

NLP Analysis Techniques
In Natural Language Processing (NLP), analysis techniques help machines interpret and process human language. Two foundational 
methods are Keyword Analysis (or Pattern Matching) and Syntactic-Driven Parsing. Each technique plays a unique role in 
understanding and extracting meaning from text.
Keyword Analysis (Pattern Matching)
Keyword analysis or pattern matching is a basic NLP technique where the system searches for specific words, 
phrases, or patterns in a text to extract information or make decisions. This method does not rely on grammatical 
structure but instead identifies predefined patterns through rules or regular expressions.
How it works:
●
Identifies relevant keywords or phrases (e.g., "weather," "temperature").
●
Uses regular expressions (regex) to match patterns (e.g., date formats like "DD/MM/YYYY").
●
Can be rule-based or template-driven for extracting information.

--- Page 20 ---
Example:
Consider the sentence:
"What is the weather in New York?"
A keyword-based system might:
●
Recognize "weather" as the topic.
●
Extract "New York" as the location.
This method works well for chatbots, search engines, and information retrieval but struggles with ambiguity and 
complex syntax (e.g., "Could you tell me how warm it is in New York?").
 Applications:
●
Chatbots: Rule-based assistants that match questions to predefined answers.
●
Named Entity Recognition (NER): Identifying entities (e.g., dates, names, places).
●
Sentiment Analysis: Matching keywords like "happy" or "angry" to detect emotions.
 Tools:
●
Regex (Regular Expressions): For advanced pattern matching.
●
SpaCy: Offers rule-based matching using Matcher and PhraseMatcher.
●
NLTK: Provides pattern-matching capabilities through regular expressions.

Syntactic-driven parsing focuses on analyzing the grammatical structure of a sentence using formal grammar rules (like 
context-free grammar). It identifies how words and phrases relate to each other, providing a structural breakdown of a 
sentence.
How it works:
●
Uses parsers to generate a parse tree representing sentence structure.
●
Applies grammar rules (e.g., Subject-Verb-Object) to identify relationships.
●
Can perform either top-down or bottom-up parsing:
○
Top-Down Parsing: Starts from a root sentence and breaks it into smaller components (e.g., S → NP + VP).
○
Bottom-Up Parsing: Starts from words and combines them into larger structures until it forms a complete 
sentence.
